NA Miata Shock Swap 1990–97 NA Miata / MX-5 · all four corners · stock-style springs & shocks

The complete shock (damper) replacement for the first-generation Miata: rear and front procedures, the long-bolt method up front, bench work with a spring compressor, and factory torque specs for every fastener you'll touch. New shocks go in; the original springs, top hats, bump stops and boots are reused after inspection at teardown. Every assembly comes apart on the bench, and nothing is torqued to spec until the corner is loaded at ride height. The alignment eccentrics are never touched, so the car keeps its alignment. Torque values are the Mazda Shop Manual ranges; where sources disagree, the disagreement is flagged rather than hidden.

01

Three golden rules

1

Compressor lives on the bench

Getting the assembly out of the car and taking it apart (spring compressor) are two separate jobs. Do both, in that order. Never compress a spring while it's still in the car.

2

Torque loaded, never at droop

Every bushing-pivot, lower-shock and end-link fastener stays snug only while the car is in the air. Final torque happens with a jack under the hub, raised until the car just starts to lift off the stand.

3

Never touch the eccentric bolts

The camber/alignment eccentrics stay untouched in every method here — that's what preserves the alignment. Loosening them is the one move that turns a Saturday job into a mandatory alignment-shop visit.

Senior-tech consult — Keith Tanner, Flyin' Miata: “Spring compressors should only be used away from the car. They're not a band-aid to get the parts into the car. Just do it the right way.” Grassroots Motorsports forum — on why the long-bolt method beats splitting ball joints. Read the thread
02

Game plan for the day

1–2 days ahead

Soak everything

Penetrating oil on every fastener you'll touch — lower shock bolts, end links, top-hat nuts. A day or two ahead is the single biggest time-saver in every guide.

First

Prep & lift

Lugs cracked loose on the ground, shaft nuts loosened a few turns, the end you're doing up on stands, wheels off, springs marked.

The work

Rears, then fronts

No steering, no long bolt — the rears are the friendly end of this car and teach the whole routine (~2 hrs for both, first time). Bench-swap each assembly, torque loaded. Then the fronts by the long-bolt method.

End of day

Wrap-up

Settle drive, fastener re-check, ride height eyeballed side-to-side, alignment check booked.

Doing just one end? That's fine — the rear and front procedures below each stand alone. Budget ~2 hours for the rears, ~3½ for the fronts, first time.

03

Tools & parts — stage them before you start

Tools

  • 21 mm socket + 21 mm box wrench — the front long bolt. The tool people forget; one forum poster lost a 2-hour round trip buying them mid-job. Do not start the front job without both.
  • 17 mm sockets, regular + deep-well; 17 mm wrench — lower shock bolts, shaft nut (stock-type), hub-carrier bolts
  • 14 mm sockets, regular + deep-well; 14 mm wrench — top-hat nuts (2 per corner), sway-bar end links. Deep-well earns its keep on the upper nuts
  • 12 mm socket — front sway-bar bracket rear bolt; ABS sensor & wire-bracket bolts (front steps)
  • 10 mm socket — undertray / splash-guard bolts if disturbed
  • Two torque wrenches — ⅜″ drive covering ~5–75 ft-lb and ½″ drive covering ~25–150 ft-lb. Most of this job lives at 22–69 ft-lb; the big wrench's headroom covers the front long bolt (up to 102 ft-lb)
  • Coil spring compressor, pair of external hooks with safety pins — most auto-parts stores loan or rent these free; inspect threads and hooks before use, lubricate the threads
  • Floor jack (low-profile) + four jack stands + wheel chocks — the jack does double duty loading each corner for final torque. A second jack or scissor jack for holding a corner is a genuine luxury
  • Breaker bar (½″), ratchets, extensions, universal/swivel joint — NASA: “There isn't much room for an impact wrench up top. You need to use a swivel.” Never loosen with a torque wrench — it ruins calibration
  • Bench vise — holds the shock body for bench work; pad the jaws with wood/aluminium if the shock is being reused, never clamp the polished piston rod
  • ½″ impact wrench — removal help only — for breaking seized fasteners. Never to drive the spring compressor, run down a shaft nut to torque, or final-tighten anything
  • Pass-through / crowfoot socket or box wrench + small wrench or hex key — counter-holding the shock shaft at its tip while the centre nut is turned
  • Pry bar, large flat-blade screwdriver, rubber mallet + hammer, needle-nose/curved pliers, magnetic pick-up tool — levering arms, ABS clip, and retrieving the top-hat nut you will otherwise drop into the body
  • Paint marker / tape, phone camera, parts trays, spare cotter pins — spring orientation, stack order, left/right discipline
  • Safety glasses, gloves, good shop light / headlamp

Parts & consumables — buy before the car goes up

  • New shocks ×4 (or ×2 for one end) — stock-type replacements: Koni, Bilstein, KYB, Tokico and the like. NA fitment is one 1990–97 application per end. Keep the maker's instruction sheet from the box — it governs the shaft nut and any settings (see the note below)
  • Bump stops ×4 — inspect first, reuse if sound — the plan is to transfer your originals. At teardown, if they're crumbled: stop and order — OEM rear stopper NA01-28-111, or the 5X Racing Street kit ($90, for stock springs). On an NA the stop is a working suspension part, not a dust cap — the car does not go back together without sound ones
  • Dust boots / covers ×4 — reuse if intact — originals are often “very brittle and broken into many sections.” If yours have disintegrated, they join the same fallback order as the stops. Some boots integrate the bump stop — check before ordering both
  • Sway-bar end links, with hardware — one pair per end on standby — the single most likely part to be seized solid or destroyed on removal
  • Top hats, rubber insulators/bushings, washers, spring-seat insulators — reused — your originals transfer if the rubber isn't cracked, separated or mushroomed and the studs are sound; replace only what fails inspection
  • Centre shaft nuts ×4 + top-hat nuts ×8 — one shaft nut per shock, two top-hat nuts per corner (×4 shocks / ×8 total; halve for one end only). Shaft nuts usually come with new shocks. The flanged 14 mm top-hat nuts are not a hardware-store item — don't lose them; have spares if corroded
  • White/clear plastic top-mount gaskets ×4 — one per corner (×2 for one end only); reuse if intact; the most-forgotten part in every written account of this job
  • Lower shock bolts/nuts (rear) — inspect and replace if threads are corroded or the bolt is bent; a bolt that needed a breaker bar and hammer to leave shouldn't automatically go back
  • Penetrating oil, molybdenum grease, wire brush, brake-clean, cable ties — grease is specified for the lower shock bolt on reassembly; cable ties re-secure the ABS wire on shocks with no welded clip
  • Springs — reused — your originals transfer onto the new shocks; keep left and right strictly separated and marked. Never mix NA/NB springs, perches and hats piecemeal

⚠ Know your shocks before assembly

Check the box and the maker's sheet before you build anything up. Adjustable shocks (Koni Sport yellows, for example): perch position and damping setting get chosen before the assemblies are built, from the maker's sheet — don't invent a setting on the day. Fixed shocks (Koni STR.T, KYB and similar): fit-and-forget, no settings to make. Either way, the maker's sheet governs the shaft nut.

⚠ Anti-seize & threadlocker note

Miata.net specifies grease on the lower shock bolt; Robinette says “use Lock-Tite on the nuts and bolts.” Don't do both on the same fastener — and remember lubricant changes the torque you actually achieve. Follow the guide for that specific fastener.

04

Prep — before anything comes off

  1. 1

    Soak every fastener Start a day ahead

    Penetrating oil, generously, on the hardware of each end you'll do: lower shock bolts, sway-bar end-link bolts/nuts, top-hat nuts (in the trunk at the rear, in the engine bay at the front). mazda-miata.nl: “Start a day or two beforehand. Lubricate all nuts and bolts of the dampers, torsion bars and carrier arms generously with penetrating oil.”

  2. 2

    Crack the lug nuts on the ground

    Park level, parking brake on, chock a wheel at the end staying down. Loosen the lug nuts on the end you're about to do while the car is on the ground — much harder and less safe once it's in the air.

  3. 3

    Loosen each shaft nut a few turns — do NOT remove it Safety

    Open the trunk (rear) or engine bay (front) and back off each shock's centre shaft (piston-rod) nut a few turns only, while the car still holds it. Off the car, this nut is far harder to loosen — and removing it now “would allow the spring to separate from the shock (which is dangerous)” (Miata.net). Spring load plus the mounted hat also resist the shaft spinning — this is your first defence against that fight later.

  4. 4

    Lift, support, wheels off

    Raise the end you're working on — under the rear differential or the front crossmember (wood pad under the saddle) — set two stands at the reinforced sill jacking points, lower onto them, and shake the car firmly before any wheel comes off. “MAKE SURE THE CAR IS SECURE! Having a car fall on you is no fun.” Details in Safety. Work one end at a time; the other end stays on the ground.

  5. 5

    Mark and photograph everything

    Trays out, tape and marker ready. Photograph and mark each spring's orientation (factory paint mark, or your own tape mark) before removal. Work one side at a time and keep left/right springs, hats and hardware together — the springs at each end are a matched pair, not spares for each other.

05

Rear shocks — the easier end, start here

“The rear shocks are easier than the fronts. Starting with these will give you a good idea of what to expect without being too difficult.” — Bobshome / San Diego Miata Club account, ~2 hours for both rears the first time. Do the rears first: they teach the whole routine — access, bench swap, loaded torque — before the fronts add the long bolt and steering hardware.

  1. 1

    Trunk access — and the left-side fuel hoses Don't skip

    Remove the spare tyre, then the metal trunk guard — it protects the fuel lines on the left and the spare well on the right — to expose the two 14 mm top-hat nuts per side. Left rear only: the fuel filler and evaporative hoses must come off to reach and torque the top nuts (NASA/Penske, stated twice). Work gently, no sparks or flames near the filler, and refit hoses and clamps exactly as found. The big filler hose lives behind that left trunk panel, sealed by hose clamps.

  2. 2

    Remove the two 14 mm top-hat nuts Two people

    Shaft nut stays put (loosened in prep). Use a magnetic socket or a finger under each nut, partner's hand or a rag underneath: “Be careful not to drop the nuts into the sheet metal around the upper mounting area, or they'll wedge into place against the body in a very tight area (especially the rear one). I dropped one, and it took about 1 hour to retrieve it.” — Miata.net

  3. 3

    Disconnect the sway bar at the bar-to-link joint

    14 mm bolt and nut — wrench on one side, socket on the other. Disconnect at the joint between the horizontal bar and the curved end link, not the link-to-A-arm joint, which Miata.net found “very difficult to reattach.” Keep fingers clear: Bobshome's link snapped back and pinched the holding wrench against the spring when the pressure released. (NASA notes one side disconnected is enough droop to extract the assembly; the bar itself needn't come off.)

  4. 4

    Remove the 17 mm lower shock bolt

    Note which way the bolt head points — it goes back the same way. Expect this to be the seized one: Robinette needed penetrating oil, a ½″ impact at 120 psi, a breaker bar and hammer taps on the rears. If it hangs up on the shock eye, raising or lowering the arm a fraction with a jack wiggles it free.

  5. 5

    Free the hub and swing it down Two people

    Two accepted variants: (a) remove the bolt joining the upper A-arm to the wheel hub and let the hub swing down (torque in the table, rear only), or (b) partner steps on the hub to lower it while you pull the assembly out — “two people make this easier.” Either way: never let the hub hang by, or lever against, the brake hose, and support the hub with wood or a jack once the shock is out.

  6. 6

    Save the white plastic gasket Most-forgotten part

    Recover the thin white/clear plastic top-mount gasket from the top of the mounting plate. Every guide repeats this because everyone forgets it. Forum tip: tape it to the fender so it can't be left behind. NASA figures it's there for NVH — reuse it.

  7. 7

    To the bench

    Disassemble and rebuild the unit on the bench with the spring compressor — full sequence in Bench work. Then reinstall per the next step.

  8. 8

    Reinstall — snug first, torque loaded

    Gasket in place, assembly offered up, top-hat studs through the chassis holes, two 14 mm nuts finger-tight only — the wiggle room lets you land the lower bolt. Partner lowers the hub (or a jack under the arm) while you guide the lower eye over the control-arm hump and insert the lower bolt greased with molybdenum grease, original orientation. Refit the upper-arm-to-hub bolt and end-link — snug, not final. Then load the corner: jack under the hub/lower arm, as close to the hub as possible, raised until the car slightly lifts off the jack stand. Now torque, in order: lower shock bolt → top-hat nuts → centre shaft nut (values below). Final end-link torque happens with the car back on the ground.

  9. 9

    Close up, repeat on the other side

    Refit the trunk guard, fuel filler/evap hoses (left side), spare and wheel. “It will go much faster since you know what you're doing.”

06

Front shocks — the long-bolt method

Three ways exist to get a front assembly out. Only one is the right default for a stock car in a home garage:

AUse this one

Long bolt

Slide out the 21 mm upper-control-arm through-bolt; the assembly swings out through the upper arm. Preserves alignment, breaks no ball joints, and is the only method that reliably fits a taller (e.g. NB) assembly back in.

Cost: you need 21 mm socket + wrench, and a seized long bolt can eat an hour.

BSuperseded

Compress on the car

Miata.net's original method: fit the compressor with the assembly still in the car. Mostly Miata tried it and found their compressor “will not fit while the front shocks are still on the car” — and Keith Tanner's rule says compressors belong on the bench anyway.

CLast resort

Hub-carrier split

Unbolt the hub carrier from the lower A-arm (Bobshome's method). Works, but disturbs more fasteners, risks ball-joint boots and the brake hose, and those bolts are “more prone to corrosion and… a hassle to realign” (Tanner). Only if the long bolt won't move. Have spare cotter pins.

Common first steps — all methods

  1. 1

    Top access in the engine bay

    Remove the plastic dust covers from the upper mounting plates; you may need to unbolt and move the diagnostic connector and wiring a few inches. On some cars an aftermarket intake blocks the driver's-side top nuts and has to come off. (European-guide note: a large washer tank can block access on some MX-5s — the smaller US Miata tank is not in the way. Check your car, don't assume.) Shaft nut is already loosened from prep; loosen the two 14 mm top-hat nuts but leave one started until you're ready to drop the assembly — it keeps your hands free.

  2. 2

    Disconnect BOTH front end links

    14 mm, both sides. Miata.net: this “will allow the lower A-arm to drop sufficiently.” NASA says the same — detach the front links so both lower arms can droop.

  3. 3

    ABS cars: free the wire and sensor ABS only

    Remove the bolt holding the ABS wire bracket to the shock / free the wire from its welded clip with a large flat-blade screwdriver. Robinette's fuller version: 12 mm ABS sensor bolt, twist the sensor out by hand, two 12 mm wire-bracket bolts on the upper A-arm, and the brake-line spring clip off (tap from below with hammer + screwdriver, or pliers). Do not strain the brake hose or ABS wire at any point from here on.

  4. 4

    Remove the 17 mm lower shock bolt

    Record the orientation — reassembly is specific: insert the bolt from the rear, nut on from the front. NASA: “Trying to put the bolt in from the front won't work. The outer tie rod is in the way.” mazda-miata.nl agrees: “the bolt head must face the rear of the car.” Watch for the lower-eye spacer collars/O-rings slipping off as it comes out.

Method A — long bolt, step by step

  1. 5

    Make room: loosen the sway-bar bracket bolt

    Loosen the rearmost bolt of the front sway-bar frame bracket (12 mm, on an extension) until its back is flush with the backing nut. This gives the long bolt somewhere to slide to. (It gets retightened to 13–20 ft-lb at the end.)

  2. 6

    Slide the long bolt back 21 mm + 21 mm

    21 mm box wrench on the nut, 21 mm socket on the bolt head. “Slide the bolt back through the big washer towards the sway bar frame bracket. Rock the A-arm up and down if the bolt is stuck. Once the bolt is all the way out of the A-arm, just leave it where it is.” NASA's variant is identical: you don't have to fully remove it, but sliding it is essential. It's very tight — breaker bar or pipe extension, hammer taps, never a torque wrench to loosen — and mazda-miata.nl's warning stands: use force if you must, but “avoid pushing the car off the stands!”

  3. 7

    Power-steering cars: mind the pipework

    On PS cars the pipework passes the driver's-side bolt: “It is tight but the bolt should just be able to pass.” Slide it, protect the pipes, never lever against them. (R-Package and manual-steering cars have clear space — one small mercy.)

  4. 8

    Swing the assembly out Two people

    Partner pries down / stands carefully on the hub or lower arm — brake hose not in tension — while you swing the top of the shock/spring unit out, clear of the shock tower, through the opening in the upper control arm, and lift the whole still-assembled unit out. Tape the fender lip if it's close (Bobshome scraped one). Support the freed upper arm and hub — don't let them hang.

  5. 9

    Bench it, then reinstall in reverse

    Disassemble/rebuild on the bench (next section). Reinstall: drop the assembly in through the upper-arm opening, top-hat nuts started loose, lower bolt in from the rear, long bolt slid back through arm and subframe, end links reconnected — everything snug only. Then jack under the lower arm near the hub until the car just starts to come off the stand and torque it all, long bolt and shaft nut included, at that loaded position. Miata.net's Hollis note, verbatim in substance: “Do not tighten mounting bolts for sway bar end links, shock lower mount or upper control arm while the car is in full droop.”

07

Bench work — the spring compressor routine

The stack, top to bottom

  1. Chassis / shock tower — the hat bolts up against it.
  2. White/clear plastic gasket — sits above the hat, against the body. The part everyone forgets.
  3. Centre shaft nut + retaining hardware — the nut that holds the whole stack together. Loosened in the car, removed only after compression.
  4. Top hat / upper mounting plate — two studs on a stock NA hat, with its rubber insulator, bushings and washers in their original order. Hats are usually reused; they're not included with a new shock.
  5. Bump stop + dust boot — the inspection gate: your originals transfer only if they're intact. Crumbled stops or disintegrated boots stop the build — the fallback order (OEM NA01-28-111 / 5X Racing Street kit) goes in and nothing gets forced together.
  6. Spring — lower end seats in the indentation/step of the lower perch; marked orientation restored. Rear springs: small end on the shock, large end on the cap.
  7. Shock — lower spring perch at the bottom, mounting eye below it.

Photograph your stack before you pull it apart. Small washers and spacers are the parts everyone loses, and the order is the reassembly instructions.

  1. 1

    Clamp the old shock body in the vise

    Body only — never the polished piston rod. If the shock is scrap, no need to protect it; if it's being reused, pad the jaws with wood or aluminium.

  2. 2

    Fit the compressor hooks

    Two hooks directly opposite each other, seated fully on coils one coil inboard from the top and bottom coils, safety pins engaged. Threads lubricated. Full safety drill in Safety.

  3. 3

    Compress — alternately, hand tools only No impact gun

    A half-turn at a time per side, keeping the spring straight and the assembly's top and bottom pointed away from both of you. Compress only until the top mounting plate is loose and the spring can rotate independently — no further. Over-compression bows the spring and walks the hooks.

  4. 4

    Now remove the shaft nut

    Already loosened in the car. If it spins the shaft, counter-hold per Gotcha 4. Fingers clear of the cap and spring; if the nut still fights on its last threads, compress a little more — fully unloaded, “the nut will turn by hand — but use a wrench just to be safe” (Robinette).

  5. 5

    Lift the stack off, in order

    Top hat → upper spring seat/insulator → bump stop/boot (inspect both now — the gate in the stack notes above) → the still-compressed spring. Keep the compressor on the spring if the same spring goes straight onto the new shock.

  6. 6

    Build the new unit

    Prime a new gas shock per the maker's sheet in the box (typically cycle the rod slowly a few times, upright). Slide on the lower perch insulator, the inspected-and-passed (or new) bump stop and dust boot, then the compressed spring — lower end seated in the perch indentation, marked orientation restored.

  7. 7

    Index the top hat

    Upper seat, hat rubber/bushings and washers in the photographed order, then the hat rotated so both studs drop through the body holes with the marked side facing the centre of the car. Robinette: line the studs up with the tower holes — “make the holes line up.” If the hat won't sit flat, the spring end isn't in its perch step: stop and re-index. Do not pull it down with the nut, and don't force it 180° out.

  8. 8

    Start the new shaft nut, counter-held

    Use the new nut supplied with the shock where supplied. Snug it while counter-holding the shaft. Final torque comes later, loaded, in the car — unless the shock maker specifies a bench torque, in which case use the shaft-nut value from the table, still counter-held, never by spinning the rod.

  9. 9

    Release the compressor slowly

    Evenly, alternating sides, checking as tension returns that both spring ends stay seated and the boot/bump stop aren't trapped or twisted. Then a rotate-and-tug check of the finished assembly. Adjustable shocks: set the adjuster accessibly — Bobshome pointed rear adjuster knobs to the rear, fronts to the front.

Finish line — when the last corner is done

Wheels on, lugs snug in a star pattern, car lowered, lugs torqued on the ground (65–87 ft-lb). Bounce each corner, roll the car or take a short gentle drive around the block to settle the suspension, check ride height side-to-side, and confirm no fastener was left merely snug. Book a four-wheel alignment check once the car has settled — within a few hundred miles. Re-check lug torque and the accessible shock fasteners after ~50–100 miles.

Torque specs — every fastener touched

Torque it
loaded

Every value below is applied with that corner jacked up under the hub until the car just starts to come off the stand — except lug nuts (on the ground) and end links (final torque on the ground). Torquing a bonded-rubber bushing pivot at full droop makes droop the bushing's neutral angle: it raises the car, adds unwanted spring rate, squeaks, and tears early.

Why Robinette's numbers are flagged, not followed

Rob Robinette's teardown is honest and useful — especially on seized fasteners — but its torque lists describe a tower with “all four 14 mm nuts,” “three pointed bolts” and separate inner/outer cap nuts. A stock NA top hat has two studs/nuts per corner. His values are shown for transparency and must not override the Miata.net factory ranges on an NA.

09

Gotchas — the eleven ways this job bites

1Seized end links — plan to replace them

Small, exposed, 30 years old. Soak for days, hold the bolt head with a box wrench while turning the nut (a socket alone spins it), and expect at least one link to need cutting off. Have new links on the bench before the car goes up — a stranded, half-done car is the failure mode here.

2The lower shock bolt — the other seized one

Robinette, rear: “Mine were seized up pretty good. I had to squirt and soak them with Liquid Wrench to break them loose even with a 1/2 inch impact wrench @ 120 psi… On the right rear I had to use a big 1/2 inch breaker bar and tap it with a hammer a few times (I even stood on the wrench handle).” Grease it on reassembly so the next person — you — can remove it.

3Captive nut spinning

If a lower bolt turns forever without tightening or loosening, the square captive nut inside the wishbone has snapped its weld. MX5OC: “Remove the wishbone and hold the nut with a spanner. It is not easy but it is doable… The nut is square not hexagon.” One restoration thread describes the same failure destroying a control arm. Prevention: days of penetrating oil, steady hand pressure to break loose, work the bolt back-and-forth once it moves — don't just gun it with an impact.

4Shaft spins with the nut — counter-hold it right

First defence: it was loosened in the car during prep. On the bench, counter-hold the shaft tip itself — the flats/hex/hex-socket at the very top of the rod — with a small wrench or hex key while turning the nut with a box wrench, crowfoot or pass-through socket. Never clamp the polished rod with Vise-Grips: scoring it means the new shock leaks. Koni trap: NA Koni Sports have “no flats or other means of holding the shaft” — decide your hold (strap/rubber protection above the seal sweep, or a shaft-hold tool) before the spring is compressed, and never use an impact to torque. A loose shaft nut in service isn't harmless either — one Grassroots thread traced ovalled top-mount holes and rattling to exactly that.

5Bump stops: inspect first — but never run without them

Mostly Miata's 280,000-mile car: “both [rears] had only remnants of bump stops remaining inside the dust boots.” Bobshome's boots were “very brittle and broken into many sections.” The NA has little bump travel and rides its stops in hard use — running without them bottoms the shock and can damage the damper and top mount. The plan in this guide is inspect-and-reuse; if they're dust, the job pauses for replacements (OEM NA01-28-111 or the 5X Racing Street kit) rather than reassembling without stops. Gluing a crumbled stop to a new boot is a get-home measure, not a fix.

6Indexing: perch, hat, gasket

Spring marked before removal, mark restored. Lower spring end in the perch indentation — an end sitting on the step raises that corner and can unseat. Rear springs: small end on the shock, large end on the cap; front and rear springs never swap ends of the car. Hat rotated until both studs drop through with the marked side facing the car's centre. And the white gasket goes back on top of the hat — tape it to the fender during removal.

7Rear access: trim, fuel filler, dropped nuts

Metal guard off (fuel lines left, spare right); left side additionally needs the fuel filler + evap hoses moved to reach and torque the nuts. Trunk trim clips are brittle at 30 years — pull them with a trim tool, expect a clip or two to break. And the top-hat nuts: drop one into the body sheet metal and you're spending an hour with curved needle-nose pliers (Miata.net's author did). Magnetic socket, partner's hand, rag in the gap.

8Brittle plastic, everywhere

Front dust caps over the top hats, the diagnostic-connector mount, trunk panels, undertray fasteners — all snap rather than flex now. Work them warm, by hand where possible, and budget replacement clips.

9Bush preload — the physics behind “torque loaded”

The NA's rubber bushes are bonded to their sleeves, so whatever arm angle exists when you clamp the bolt becomes the bush's neutral angle. Good-Win Racing: locking bolts down at full droop “can… eventually damage the bushings.” This applies to any pivot bolt you loosened, not just the shock bolts.

10Alignment afterward — required, not optional

Even though these methods avoid disturbing the eccentrics, you've changed ride height (new dampers, new bump stops, settled bushes) and had the hubs swinging free. Fat Cat lists “a full four-wheel alignment… especially if you change dampers/springs” as step 3 of any suspension refresh; RepairPal says the same. Let the car settle first — aligning a car still sitting high on twisted bushes gives a correct alignment at the wrong height.

11The small stuff that still stops you

Front lower eye stuck in its mount — pry gently with a large flat screwdriver. Shock top scraping the fender on the way out — partner guides, tape the lip. ABS wire with nowhere to go on aftermarket shocks — cable tie, with a piece of old boot as a cushion (Bobshome's fix). Gas-pressure shocks extending themselves while you fit them — wire one compressed if needed. And never use a torque wrench for loosening — you'll ruin its calibration.

★The 21 mm sockets

Worth its own box: one poster lost a 2-hour round trip mid-job just to buy 21 mm sockets for the long bolt. Front job only — but check you have them before the front wheels come off.

10

Safety — read before the first spring comes off

⚠ The spring is a loaded weapon

Robinette: “The coil springs have a considerable amount of energy stored in them, do not under any circumstances remove the spring cap without first compressing the springs. If you don't use spring compressors the cap nut will fly off at high speed in one direction, the shock will go in the other direction and the tip of the shaft will strip as the nut flies off… stock springs need a compressor.”

Never use the “explosive disassembly” shortcut that circulates in forum banter (foot on the mount, undo the nut, let it fly). Keith Tanner mentions it only to say: “Never do this, of course.”

Spring compressor drill

  • Inspect the tool first: no bent rods, damaged threads, cracked hooks. Lubricate the threads. Reject a damaged tool — including your own.
  • Glasses and gloves on. Keep the assembly's top and bottom aimed away from both of you for the entire operation. Never stand in line with the spring axis; never reach over a compressed spring.
  • Hooks directly opposite, fully seated, locking pins in.
  • Tighten alternately, ~half a turn per side, hand wrench only — The Drive: “do not use an impact gun. You want to keep the force distribution between the two as equal as possible, and an impact gun will make this very hard to do.”
  • Compress only until the top plate is loose / the spring rotates.
  • Stop immediately on creaking, slipping or bowing. If a hook walks, release tension evenly and reseat — never try to catch or reseat a loaded spring by hand.
  • Release the same way: slowly, evenly, alternating. Never leave a compressed spring unattended on the bench — finish the corner or release it.

Lifting & support on an NA

  • Stands, never a jack alone. Raise a whole end under the front crossmember or the rear differential (Lanny Chambers' method) with a wood pad under the saddle; stands go at the reinforced sill (pinch-weld) jacking points — just behind the front wheels, just ahead of the rears.
  • Use a grooved pad, slotted puck or grooved 2×4 over the seam so it isn't crushed: “Cut a groove down the length of a piece of 2x4… deep enough to straddle the lip along the bottom of the rocker panel.”
  • Rusty or crushed sill? Do not support the car there. Use the subframe/frame-rail alternative and treat the rust as a structural problem, not a jacking inconvenience.
  • Jack slowly, centre the load, set both stands, lower onto them, then shake the car firmly before any wheel comes off or anyone goes under. Chocks in, brake on.
  • Low car: check the jack fits before you need it; driving onto wood blocks first is the standard fix.
  • When loading a corner for torque, the jack under the hub is in addition to a stand positioned to catch the car — raise only until the stand just unloads, torque, lower back on.
  • No part of either person under the car while it's being raised or lowered — and the second person stays clear of the long-bolt breaker-bar arc.
11

Year & variant notes — what actually changes

  • 1990–93 (1.6, NA6) vs 1994–97 (1.8, NA8): the procedure, fasteners and torque values in this guide are the same. 1994 brought the 1.8, dual airbags, a Torsen option and substantial extra chassis bracing (including the seat-belt-tower “track bar”) — rear brace hardware can crowd rear lower-arm work on some cars, but it changes neither the procedure nor any torque value.
  • Ride height differs by year — don't judge your result against the wrong year. Mazda Motorsports' Spec Miata baselines set different pinch-weld heights for '89–'93 1.6, '94–'95 1.8 and '96–'97 1.8 cars; 1996–97 cars sit notably tall stock.
  • R-Package / 1993 LE: factory Bilsteins, stiffer springs and bars — same procedure, and no power steering, which makes the long bolt easier (no pipework in the way).
  • Power steering: pipework passes the driver's-side long bolt — slide it, protect the pipes, never lever against them.
  • ABS: front wire clipped/bracketed to the shock plus a sensor and bracket on the upper A-arm (12 mm). Many replacement shocks have no welded clip — cable-tie fix is accepted practice. Rear work unaffected. Non-ABS cars skip all of it.
  • NA vs NB parts mixing — a common “upgrade” trap: a complete NB assembly can go into an NA, but you can't freely mix NA springs (closed coil both ends), NB springs (open coil one end), NA perches and NB top hats — the hat moves the spring's mounting plane and changes ride height. Tanner: “You can put a complete NB assembly in an NA but not vice versa.” And an NB assembly won't fit in or out by the ball-joint method — long bolt only.
  • Front vs rear, in one line: front = top nuts from the engine bay, long bolt, both end links off, ABS/brake care, lower bolt in from the rear. Rear = top nuts from the trunk, fuel-filler obstruction on the left only, hub swung down, no steering parts.

Open questions — not verified, don't guess

Exact shaft-nut size/torque for your specific replacement brand (Koni, Bilstein, KYB…) varies by maker and wasn't verified per-brand — the shock in the box and its instruction sheet govern that one nut. No current Flyin' Miata or Moss written (PDF) NA install sheet with its own torque table could be verified — FM's current NA guidance is the video below plus kit-specific sheets; every torque value on this page is Miata.net/FSM, mazda-miata.nl, Buckeye, NASA or Robinette, labelled as such. The ABS bolt torque (12–16 ft-lb) rests on Robinette alone.
